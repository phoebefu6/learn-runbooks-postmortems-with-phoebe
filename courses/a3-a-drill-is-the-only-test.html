Part 1 · covers why only a drill tests a page

Reading a page is not running it

5 min live

There are three ways to check a runbook. The linter from Writer session 2 checks its shape: every action has a confirm line, every command has an expected output, no step says "check it looks fine". A review has a person read it. A drill has a person who did not write it run it, step by step, against a system. A page can score 100 on the linter and pass a careful review while step 2 names a host that was retired last month. The linter checks shape, not truth, and a reviewer reads with the writer's assumptions. Only the drill puts the page in contact with the system.

LiveWhat a drill catches that a review cannot3 min▶

The reviewer knows too much. A reviewer reads "restart the job on the scheduler" and their head fills in which scheduler, because they were there when it moved. A newcomer has nothing to fill in with, so the gap shows. The same goes for the dashboard everyone "just knows" and the step that silently assumes you are already on the bastion host.

The reviewer's access works. A page written by someone with admin rights reads perfectly to another admin. The first time a new on-call engineer runs it, step 4 returns "permission denied" at 2 a.m. Only someone running the page with an ordinary on-call account finds that.

The system changed since the read. A command, a hostname, a dashboard link: all of these are claims about the system, and a read cannot test a claim. Running the step does. That is why the linter score from Writer session 2 is necessary but never enough: it says a page can be checked, and the drill is the check.

Self-studyPaper drill vs live game day4 min▶

Paper drill (tabletop). The reader walks the page aloud while a facilitator plays the system: "you run that command, here is what comes back". It is cheap, safe and good at finding vague steps and missing context. It cannot find a drifted command, because the facilitator answers from memory, and memory is exactly what drifted.

Live drill on a safe system. The reader runs the real commands against a staging copy, or against an instance diverted from live traffic, with a fault injected. The SRE book's training chapter recommends exactly this for its exercises, in the section titled "Break Real Things, Fix Real Things": realism matters, so borrow an instance that can be taken out of live traffic, or a small staging instance, and break it on purpose. It costs more setup and finds what a tabletop cannot.

Live game day. A real fault on a production system, under control. The most realistic and the most expensive; most teams run it a few times a year for their most critical pages. Pellmoor's one ad hoc game day in week 6 of the constructed quarter drilled 3 pages, which is where the bench's starting drill coverage comes from.

Pick per page. A page whose steps are mostly commands needs a live drill; a page that is mostly judgement and escalation can be tested well on paper.

Real world

Google trains on-call engineers by re-enacting old incidents. The SRE book's chapter on bringing engineers onto on-call describes disaster role playing, which it says is commonly called Wheel of Misfortune or Walk the Plank: a game master picks an on-call engineer, presents a scenario often taken from a past outage, and supplies what the system would show while the engineer works the problem. The same chapter notes that newer engineers depend on documentation far more than seniors, who fill the gaps from memory. (Google, Site Reliability Engineering (2016), "Accelerating SREs to On-Call and Beyond", sre.google, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers timing a newcomer

Start a clock, stay silent, stop at the first failure

5 min live

A drill has a protocol, and the protocol is what makes the result mean something. Pick a reader who has never run the page. Start a clock. The reader reads each step aloud and runs it. The writer stays silent. You mark every step where the reader stops, guesses or asks. The drill ends at the first step that fails, and that step goes in the log. What a failed step teaches is always the same: fix the page, not the reader.

LiveThe drill protocol, and the drill log4 min▶

Pick the reader. Someone on the rota who has never run this page. Not the writer, not the owner, not the person who fixed this alert last month. The newest on-call engineer is the best reader you have.

Start the clock and read aloud. Reading aloud makes every guess audible. "I think this means the second dashboard" is a finding, even if the guess was right.

The writer stays silent. Every hint the writer gives is a step the page is missing, and the hint will not be in the room at 2 a.m. If the reader is truly stuck, they say so, you mark the step, and the drill ends there.

Stop at the first failed step. A step fails when the reader cannot finish it from the page alone: the command errors, the link is dead, access is denied, or they had to ask. Everything after it is untested, so the log says "not reached", not "passed".

Write the log row. Date in UTC, page, reader (a role, not a name), time per step, first failed step, the fix, and a re-drill date. The time per step matters as much as the failure: a step that took six minutes and passed is next quarter's failure.

Self-studyA drill tests the reader too, and that is not the point3 min▶

The SRE book's training chapter lists, among its anti-patterns, training "strictly through operator procedures, checklists, and playbooks" (Google, Site Reliability Engineering, "Accelerating SREs to On-Call and Beyond"). Its point is that a playbook does not replace understanding the system: an engineer who can only follow steps is lost the moment the steps run out.

So a drill always tests two things at once: whether the page is true and complete, and whether the reader understands enough to work when it is not. Keep them apart in the log. The page is the subject of the drill, and the reader is not being graded. When a reader stalls, the first question is always what the page failed to say. A separate question, asked kindly and outside the log, is what the reader needs to learn about the system, and that is a training plan, not a drill finding.

Teams that grade readers in drills soon find nobody volunteers to read. Teams that fix pages find the newest engineers asking to be drilled, because it is the fastest way to learn the system with a safety net.

Real world

Constructed Pellmoor example: the scheduler that moved. RB-24 had an owner, passed the linter, and its owner had reviewed it after the last two picking changes. The scheduler migration that broke step 2 was filed against the job platform, not the picking system, so the change-triggered review never routed it to RB-24's owner. The drill found it in six minutes on a Tuesday afternoon. Without the drill, a picking on-call would have found it at the start of the early wave with vans waiting.

Part 3 · covers the drill rota

Every paged page, once a quarter

5 min live

One game day drills a handful of pages. A rota drills all of them. After the inventory, Pellmoor has 31 paged pages. Drilling each once a quarter spreads them across 13 weeks: 31 / 13 is about 2.4, so two or three drills a week, each needing one reader and one silent observer. That is a rota a team can keep, and on the bench it takes the library from 14% misleading to 5%.

LiveBuilding the rota3 min▶

Start from the inventory, not the wiki. The rota covers paged pages: every page a paging alert sends a human to. On Pellmoor that is 31, the 25 that were bound when the inventory started plus the 6 written for alerts that had no page. Unpaged pages, such as a month-end close checklist, are not on this rota; they need their own, slower plan.

Spread, do not batch. 31 pages over 13 weeks is about 2.4 a week. A grid with weeks across and systems down shows at a glance when one system is drilled three weeks running and another not at all. Spread each system across the quarter so a big change in week 5 is followed by a drill a few weeks later.

Pair the rota with the on-call roster. The newest person on next week's roster reads; the page owner observes in silence and fixes. A failed drill earns a re-drill date in the same quarter, which is why the rota leaves slack weeks with only two slots.

Self-studyDrilling harder does not replace review3 min▶

A natural question: if a drill catches every wrong step, why not skip change-triggered review and drill more often? The bench answers it. With owners and rewrites but no change-triggered review, and a drill every 4 weeks instead of once a quarter, Pellmoor ends at "6 of 44 pages would mislead at 2 a.m. (14%)", the same as review with no rota at all, and well above review plus a quarterly drill.

The reason is timing. A drill is a snapshot: it finds what is wrong on the day it runs. A change that breaks a step the week after the drill stays broken until the next one. Review on change catches most breaks close to when they happen; the drill then catches what review missed, including the breaks that were never routed to the right owner. They do different jobs, and the cheaper one, review, does most of the lifting. The workbook's on-call chapter makes the same pairing from the other side: playbook details go out of date as fast as production changes, so the page has to be touched when the system is.

Real world

Why a true page is worth the effort: the reported 3x. The SRE book's introduction reports that writing best practice down ahead of time in a playbook gave roughly a 3x improvement in mean time to repair compared with improvising. That is Google's own reported experience; the book gives no method, so treat it as a practitioner's report, not a measured effect. Notice the condition hidden in it: the gain comes from pages that are true when read, and the drill is how you know. (Google, Site Reliability Engineering (2016), Introduction, sre.google, read at source; reported experience.)

Homework

Before Writer session 4

30 min
  1. Run one drill. Pick a paged page bound to an alert that fires often, and a reader who has never run it. Clock on, writer silent, stop at the first failed step.
  2. Write the log row. Date in UTC, page, reader as a role, time per step, first failed step, fix, re-drill date.
  3. Fix the page, then re-drill. Rewrite the failed step against the system as it is now, and book the re-drill with a different reader.
  4. Draft your rota. Count your paged pages, divide by 13, and lay them out on a weeks-by-systems grid with slack weeks for re-drills.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A page scores 100 on the linter and its owner reviewed it last week. Which wrong step can only a drill catch?

The linter catches vague steps and missing confirm or expected lines. A drifted host and a missing permission are claims about the system; only running the step tests them.

2 · Why does the writer stay silent during a drill?

The drill tests the page. A hint fills a gap the page should fill, and hides it from the log.

3 · With a quarterly rota, R4 still leaves 2 of 44 pages that would mislead. Why?

On the bench a drill catches every wrong step on the page it runs. Coverage is of paged pages only, so unpaged pages need their own plan.
