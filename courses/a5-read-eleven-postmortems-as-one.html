Part 1 · covers the corpus as a dataset

One postmortem explains one incident; a year of them shows a pattern

5 min live

A postmortem is written to close one incident. Read alone, a silent job failure in week 6 looks like bad luck in the finance warehouse. Read beside a silent job failure in week 10 in warehouse picking, and three more later in the year, it stops looking like luck. The corpus is the same documents with one extra field: what conditions made each incident possible, written in words the next reader can count.

LiveWhat a corpus answers that one write-up cannot3 min▶

Frequency. Which conditions sit behind most incidents. One postmortem gives you a count of one for everything it names.

Repeats. Whether this incident happened before, and what the earlier write-up promised to do about it. A repeat is invisible from inside the second postmortem unless someone goes looking in the first.

Follow-through. How many of the actions you wrote across the year actually got done, and how old the open ones are. Each postmortem shows its own actions; only the corpus shows the closing rate.

None of this needs new writing. It needs the postmortems in one place, a short list of condition tags applied to each, and someone who reads them together on a schedule.

Self-studyWhat this session does not reteach2 min▶

Writing the single postmortem is Writer session 4. What a postmortem may conclude, and why blameless is the entry condition, lives in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗. The package one incident generates (record, timeline, impact) is in Data Reliability, the incident package ↗. Here, each of those documents is a row in a table, and the table is the thing you read.

Self-studyA public corpus, sorted by condition2 min▶

The danluu/post-mortems index ↗ on GitHub is a long public list of incident write-ups. Look at how it is organised before you read any entry: not by company or by date, but by the kind of condition behind the failure, under headings such as Config Errors, Hardware/Power Failures, Conflicts, Time and Database, with an Uncategorized bucket for the rest. Config errors is the largest named group, about 60 entries by a hand count. That choice of grouping is the same move you make tonight: the useful axis for a pile of postmortems is the condition, not the team.

The index states no licence, so treat it as a reading list: follow the links, and quote nothing from it into your own documents.

Real world

Google treats its postmortems as data. The SRE workbook's postmortem chapter describes a searchable repository of thousands of postmortems going back to 2009, analysed for trends such as postmortems per month, time to detect and resolve, and blast radius. The same chapter lists repeating incidents among the warning signs of a postmortem culture that is not working. A repository you can query is what turns repeats from an anecdote into a count. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook (2018), ch. 10 "Postmortem Culture", sre.google, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers tagging conditions

A short vocabulary, applied to every write-up

5 min live

You cannot count what every author names differently. "The nightly load died quietly", "no alert on the refund job" and "pick-list generation failed without error" are the same condition in three dialects. A controlled vocabulary is a short list of condition tags the whole team uses; each postmortem gets one to three of them, chosen at review; and the count across the year is a sum, not an argument.

LiveBuild the vocabulary small, and keep it small3 min▶

Start from what you already wrote. Read last year's contributing-conditions sections and write each condition as a short noun phrase about the system, never about a person: "silent job failure", "stale runbook step", "unvalidated supplier input", "deploy without rollback check", "queue alert too late", "credits not watched". These are Pellmoor's, and they read like conditions an engineer could fix.

Tag one to three per postmortem. More than three and every tag gets diluted; the count stops meaning "this mattered here". Pellmoor's reviewers settled on two each.

Tag at the review, not later. The people who just argued about contributing conditions are the right people to choose the tags. A tag added by someone reading the doc cold six months later is a guess.

Prune the vocabulary on a schedule. A tag used once in a year is either a rare condition or a synonym for one you already have. Merge synonyms; keep genuine rarities; retire tags nobody uses.

Self-studyTwo taggers will disagree, and that is the point of reviewing the list3 min▶

Tagging a condition is a judgement, not a measurement. Was PM-10, the refund job that skipped a day, a "silent job failure" or "no alert on silent job"? Pellmoor's reviewers gave it both, which is defensible, and another team would have chosen one. Practitioners disagree about how strict to be: one camp wants a fixed taxonomy so counts compare year on year; another argues a fixed list pushes new kinds of failure into the nearest wrong box. Neither side has a measured answer you can borrow.

What you can do is make the disagreement visible. Keep the vocabulary short enough that two people can learn it, write one sentence defining each tag, and when a count drives a decision, have a second person re-tag the postmortems behind it before you act. If they land somewhere else, you have learned the tag is fuzzy, which is worth knowing before you spend a quarter on it.

The tags are contributing conditions, not root causes. Whether a postmortem should name a single root cause at all is itself contested; Data Reliability, postmortems ↗ lays out that argument.

Real world

A public index that sorts by condition. The danluu/post-mortems README groups its entries under condition headings (Config Errors, Hardware/Power Failures, Conflicts, Time, Database, Uncategorized) rather than by company or year. Even its Uncategorized bucket is a lesson: some incidents resist a short vocabulary, and a good index admits it instead of forcing them into a box. (danluu/post-mortems, GitHub index, structure read at source, no licence stated.)

Part 3 · covers the repeat incident

A wish lets the same incident through twice

5 min live

Two of Pellmoor's eleven postmortems describe incidents that had already happened. Both trace back to one action in the earlier postmortem, and both of those actions were written as wishes: a direction with no owner or no date, and no way to tell when it is done. A wish feels like progress in the review meeting and closes nothing afterwards. The corpus is where you see it, because the repeat sits in a different document, weeks later, written by someone who may never have read the first.

LiveRead the two repeats3 min▶

Constructed Pellmoor example. In week 10, PM-03 records the morning pick lists missing for the warehouse wave: the pick-list job failed and said nothing. Among its actions is PM-03.2, "Look into the pick-list job". No owner, no date, and nothing that says what "looked into" would look like. In week 36, PM-09 is titled "Pick lists missing again". Same system, same two tags: silent job failure, stale runbook step.

In week 14, PM-04 records a supplier feed schema change that broke validation. Its first action, PM-04.1, is "Make validation better". It has an owner, but no date, and "better" has no finish line. In week 47, PM-11 records wrong prices shown to customers for 3 hours. The tag it shares with PM-04 is unvalidated supplier input.

Neither repeat is a surprise once the two documents sit side by side. Both are invisible if you only ever read the newest one.

LiveWish or action: the test is whether anyone can say it is done2 min▶

An action has one owner, a date, and a definition of done someone else could check. A wish has a verb like look into, improve, be more careful, think about. The SRE workbook's good postmortem gives every action a type, a priority, one owner and a tracking bug, and notes that changing human behaviour is less reliable than changing automated systems, which is why "be more careful with deploys" belongs in the wish pile. PagerDuty's guide asks for actions that are actionable, specific and bounded in scope. Both say the same thing from different sides: if nobody can tell when it is finished, it will not be.

The linter in Writer session 4 flags wish verbs in a postmortem's actions. That is a check of shape, not of truth: a clean action can still be the wrong action, or the right action nobody does.

Self-studyWhy fixing the wording alone does not stop the repeat3 min▶

On the bench, rung R2 rewrites every wish into an action with an owner and a date. The wish count drops to zero. The closing count does not move, and both repeats still happen. That is the model saying out loud what many teams find the hard way: a well-formed action in a document nobody checks is still an open action. In Pellmoor's history, nobody ever came back to PM-03.2 or PM-04.1, so rewriting them changed the words and not the weeks.

What closes them is a rung you have not met yet: someone who checks open actions on a cadence. Writer session 6 runs it on the bench, and Leader session 4, Actions that close, is about running it in a real organisation.

Real world

Google's line on unfinished postmortems. The SRE workbook quotes Ben Treynor Sloss: a postmortem "without subsequent action is indistinguishable from no postmortem". Google files action items as bugs in a central tracker so it can monitor their closure, rewards closing actions and not only writing them, and lists repeating incidents among the warning signs of a failing postmortem culture. The repeat is the visible symptom; the open action is the condition. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook (2018), ch. 10 "Postmortem Culture", sre.google, read at source.)

Homework

Before Writer session 6

30 min
  1. Gather a year. Put every postmortem your team wrote in the last twelve months in one list, with its date, severity and title.
  2. Draft a vocabulary of six to ten condition tags from their contributing-conditions sections, one sentence defining each, and tag every postmortem with one to three.
  3. Count and look for repeats. Tally the tags; for any two postmortems on the same system with a shared tag, open the earlier one and find the action that should have prevented the later.
  4. Write your cross-incident read on the Step 5 template, and ask one colleague to re-tag three of the postmortems blind. Note where you disagreed.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · What can a year of postmortems read together show that no single postmortem can?

A timeline, a responder and a linter score all live inside one document. Frequency and repeats only exist across documents, so only the corpus can show them.

2 · PM-03.2 read "Look into the pick-list job". Why did that let PM-09 through?

A wish has no finish line, so it never shows up as late. The incident it was meant to prevent arrives first.

3 · On the bench, R2 turns every wish into an action with an owner and a date. Why are repeats still 2 of 2?

R2 does change the corpus: wishes fall from 18 of 46 to 0. But closed stays at 13 of 46. Wording is necessary, not sufficient; something has to check the open actions.
