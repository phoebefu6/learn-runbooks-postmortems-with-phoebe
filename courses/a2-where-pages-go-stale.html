Where pages go stale: drift rides on change

A runbook does not rot because it is old. It goes wrong because the system under it moved: a column renamed, a host replaced, a flag flipped. The words on the page stay exactly as they were, so the page still looks fine to everyone who glances at it. Tonight you learn to review pages when the system changes rather than when the calendar says so, and to write steps that let a reviewer see the drift. Then you rewrite one of Pellmoor's runbooks until the linter passes it.

Part 1 · covers drift

Every change is a chance a step stops being true

5 min live

Google's SRE workbook puts the mechanism in one line: playbook details "go out of date at the same rate as production environment changes" (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, "On-Call"). Read it as a model, not a slogan. Each change to a system is a draw: most leave its pages alone, some quietly break one step. The page text never moves, which is why drift is invisible from the page.

LiveChange, not age3 min▶

Ask a team how stale a page is and they look at the last-edited date. That is the wrong clock. A page about a system nobody touched all year can still be true; a page about the pricing feed, which changes whenever a supplier does, can be wrong by Friday. Staleness is a function of the changes that landed on the system since someone last walked the steps, not of the months that passed.

The same workbook chapter gives the other half of the habit: when the page fires, the on-call engineer updates the playbook with what they just learned. That keeps the pages that get used close to true. The pages that are rarely used are the ones changes catch, and the ones nobody notices until 2 a.m.

So the useful question for any page is: what changed in its system since it was last verified? If the answer is "three deploys and a schema change", the page is a suspect, whatever its date says.

Self-studyWhy nobody publishes a drift rate3 min▶

You might want a number: what share of changes break a runbook step? No public measurement exists that could be borrowed. It depends on how your pages are written, how coupled they are to names that change (hosts, columns, flags) and how big your changes are. Any industry figure would describe somebody else's system.

The bench therefore simulates drift. Each change that touches a page breaks one of its steps with a probability set to 30% by default, labelled on the widget and movable with the slider. The catch rates for reviews are simulated too. What the bench claims is only the ordering: review on change beats a one-off read, a checkable page is easier to review than a vague one, and a drill is the only full test. Move the slider and the counts change; the ordering does not.

Real world

The newest responder pays for drift first. Google's SRE book, in its chapter on bringing new engineers on call, notes that in a fast-changing environment documentation can fall out of date quickly, and that newer engineers lean on it more than seniors, who fill the gaps from memory. A stale page costs the expert a shrug and the newcomer an hour. (Google, Site Reliability Engineering (2016), "Accelerating SREs to On-Call and Beyond", read at source.)

Part 2 · covers change-triggered vs calendar review

Review on change, not on the calendar

5 min live

Most teams that review runbooks at all do it on a calendar: every page, every six months, whether or not anything changed. A change-triggered review works the other way round. When a deploy, a config push or a schema change lands, the pages that describe the touched system go to their owners that week. It reads fewer pages and reads them when they are most likely to be wrong.

LiveWhat the trigger needs3 min▶

A change feed. Somewhere every deploy, config push and schema change is already recorded: the deploy pipeline, the config repository, the migration log, the supplier onboarding ticket. The trigger reads from there. If a class of change leaves no record, it cannot trigger anything, and that is worth knowing in itself.

A page-to-system map. The inventory you built in Writer session 1 already holds it: every page, the system it describes, the alert bound to it. A change to the pricing feed looks up the pricing feed's pages. Without the map, the trigger can only say "something changed", which is the calendar review again.

An owner. The touched pages go to a named role who walks the changed steps against the changed system and updates the last-verified date. A trigger with no owner on the other end is a notification nobody reads.

Self-studyGeneral entries or step-by-step? A live disagreement3 min▶

The SRE workbook's on-call chapter records a real split. Some teams keep playbook entries general, describing the system and the diagnosis rather than exact commands, because general entries change slowly and so drift less. Other teams prefer step-by-step entries, with commands and expected output, because a tired reader can follow them without having to reason from first principles. The workbook does not pick a winner; it asks each team to agree a minimal structure.

Both sides have a point that bears on tonight. General entries drift less, but when they drift nobody can tell, because there is nothing precise to check. Step-by-step entries drift more, because every hostname and column is a name a change can break, but the drift is visible to a reviewer who runs the step. Change-triggered review leans on the second property; it works best on pages precise enough to be checked. Decide with your team which kind each page is, and review each accordingly.

Real world

Constructed Pellmoor example: the ladder's biggest step. On the bench, Pellmoor's pages are linter-clean and owned at R2, and 18 of 44 still mislead at the end of the quarter. R3 changes one thing: each system change routes its touched pages to their owners. The same changes break the same steps, and the share that misleads falls to 6 of 44. Nothing about the pages improved; the reviews just happened on the right weeks.

Part 3 · covers checkable steps

Steps a reviewer can check

5 min live

A change-triggered review only works if the reviewer can tell a broken step from a working one. On a page that says "restart the adapter" and stops, they cannot: the command runs, nothing says what should come back, and the reviewer nods. Three linter rules target exactly the steps that hide drift.

LiveThree rules that make drift visible3 min▶

Step needs judgement. "Check if the error rate looks high", "investigate as appropriate". The step hands the decision to the most tired person in the building, and it hands the reviewer nothing to compare. Name the reading and the threshold.

Action with no confirm step. A restart, a failover, a re-run, with no line saying how to tell it worked. The reader moves on after an action that did nothing; the reviewer cannot tell whether the action still does anything at all.

Command with no expected output. A command with nothing after it cannot be checked, by the reader at 2 a.m. or by the reviewer who should notice that a change made it print something else. The widget shows each rule's full explanation beside its findings.

Two more rules apply to every runbook, an owner and last-verified line and UTC timestamps, and you met their reasons in Writer session 1.

Self-studyShape, not truth, and why shape still matters3 min▶

The linter is a rule engine, not a judge of truth. It reads the shape of a page: are there thresholds, confirm lines, expected outputs, an owner, UTC times. A page can score 100 and still be wrong, because the deployment it names was renamed last week. Only running the step against the system shows that, which is why Writer session 3 is about drills.

Shape still matters to drift, and the bench models how. An owner reviewing a linter-clean page catches a broken step 90% of the time; on a page that fails the rules, 40%. Both rates are simulated, shipped as fixed rolls so every browser prints the same numbers, and only their ordering is claimed: a page with expected output is easier to check than one without.

For the anatomy of a single good runbook, read Tech Writing, runbooks ↗ and Data Reliability, runbooks ↗. Here the point is narrower: checkable steps are what let a review find drift.

Real world

Constructed Pellmoor example: the restart that did nothing. The payments on-call follows RB-07 as found. Step 2 says restart the adapter; the command returns, and the page says nothing about what should happen next, so they move to step 3. Twenty minutes later they are reading a provider status page that was never the problem. With an expected output and a confirm line, the step would have told them in five minutes that the restart had not helped, and the reviewer after last month's deploy would have seen the same thing.

Step 5 · Write the change-trigger rule for one Pellmoor system. Pick the pricing feed, the order pipeline or another system from the inventory, and fill the rule so a tool or a person could run it without asking you.

★ Step 5 · change-trigger rule (template)System: ______________________ (e.g. pricing feed) Change sources: deploys from __________; config pushes to __________; schema changes from __________ Pages touched: look up the system in the inventory: RB-__, RB-__, RB-__ Route to: the owner of each page, within __ working days of the change Review means: run every step with an expected output against the changed system Done when: steps match, and "Last verified: YYYY-MM-DD" is updated If no owner: escalate to ______________________

Put the trigger where the change already is. A trigger that lives in a separate reminder will be forgotten the first busy week. Attach it to the deploy or the merge itself: a line in the change template that lists the pages it touches, or a bot that posts them to the owners when the change lands.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Two runbooks were both last edited a year ago. One describes the pricing feed, which changed weekly; the other a job nobody touched. Which is more likely to be stale, and why?

Playbook details go out of date as fast as production changes. The clock that matters is the changes since the last verification, not the months.

2 · A reviewer re-reads a page right after a deploy and passes it. Step 2 is a restart command with nothing after it. Why might they miss that the step drifted?

A step with no expected output gives the reviewer nothing to compare. Checkable steps are what let a review see drift; the linter only checks that the shape is there.

3 · On the bench, R2 makes every page owned and linter-clean and leaves 18 of 44 misleading. What does R3 add that takes it to 6 of 44?

The same 20 changes break steps at both rungs. R3 only changes when reviews happen: on the week a change lands, by the page's owner.
