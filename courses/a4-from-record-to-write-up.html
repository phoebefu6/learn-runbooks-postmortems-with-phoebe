Part 1 · covers what a postmortem is and when it is owed

What a postmortem is, and when one is owed

5 min live

Google's SRE book defines a postmortem as a written record of an incident: its impact, the actions taken to mitigate or resolve it, its causes, and the follow-up actions that stop it coming back. The close-out record already holds most of the "what happened". The write-up adds the parts nobody logs during an incident: why it was possible, and what will change.

LiveWhen a postmortem is owed3 min▶

Agree the triggers before the incident, not during the argument after it. The SRE book lists the usual ones, here in paraphrase: users saw downtime or degradation beyond an agreed threshold; any data was lost; on-call had to step in, with a rollback or a reroute; resolution took longer than an agreed threshold; or monitoring failed, which usually means a human found the incident first. A team may also ask for one on any incident it wants to learn from.

Then set a clock and one name. PagerDuty's public guide asks for a postmortem on every major incident, Sev-1 and Sev-2, and states its own internal deadlines: three calendar days for a Sev-1, five business days for a Sev-2. The incident commander picks one owner, so the write-up does not wait while everyone assumes someone else has it. One owner does not mean one author: the writing is collaborative, and the owner's job is to get it finished.

Pellmoor's PM-11 meets two triggers at once: customers saw wrong prices for 3 hours, and on-call intervened to restore the last accepted feed. That is a postmortem owed, whatever its severity label says.

Self-studyWhat a postmortem may conclude, and the entry condition2 min▶

Two things are taught properly elsewhere and only named here. What a postmortem is allowed to conclude, and why blamelessness is the condition for honest accounts rather than a courtesy, are covered in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗. A package generated from the incident's own data, so the timeline is pulled rather than remembered, is in Data Reliability, the incident package ↗. Here, the unit is the write-up itself: its sections, its sentences and its actions.

One more reader is coming. PM-11 put wrong prices in front of customers, so it also needs a public version with names and internal systems removed. That is a different document for a different reader, and Leader session 3 builds it from the team version you write tonight.

Real world

Same outage, two write-ups, months apart. The SRE workbook's postmortem chapter prints a bad and a good postmortem of the same 2014 outage. The bad one has missing context, vague actions, finger-pointing, many owners and a small audience, and it was published four months late. The good one was written and circulated less than a week after the incident closed. Four months on, the logs have rotated and the people have moved to other work; a week on, both are still within reach. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook (2018), ch. 10 "Postmortem Culture", read at source.)

Part 2 · covers timeline and impact

A timeline anyone can replay, an impact anyone can count

5 min live

A timeline is read by people who were not there, often weeks later, sometimes in another time zone. So every entry carries an absolute date and a UTC time, says what the system did, and starts before anything looked wrong. Impact is read by people deciding what to fix first, so it is counted, not described.

LiveThree rules for a timeline3 min▶

Absolute dates. "This morning" is true for one day. The postmortem is read at the review, again at the monthly reading, and again in a year when the same feed fails. Write the date every time; it costs ten characters.

UTC, labelled. "09:40" means one thing to the warehouse team and another to a supplier in a different zone, and logs from different systems are compared minute by minute. Pick UTC, say so in the heading, and write the zone on every line so a pasted line keeps its meaning.

Start before the incident and work forward. PagerDuty's blameless guidance names hindsight bias: once you know the outcome, every earlier moment looks like an obvious warning. Writing from before anything looked wrong, in the order people lived it, shows what they actually saw. In the s4 version the first line is the file arriving, not the first customer complaint.

And the subject of each line is the system or a role, never a name. "09:40 Priya pushed the feed" makes the timeline about a person; "09:40 UTC feed accepted; the validator checks the schema, not the values" makes it about the condition that let the bad file through.

Self-studyImpact as numbers, even rough ones3 min▶

Count what the reader will use to rank this incident against the others: customers affected, orders, products, minutes, money. The SRE workbook asks for impact to be quantified, and its answer to "we do not have the exact number" is that "a well-informed estimate is better than no data at all" (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10). Say it is an estimate and say how you made it.

Pellmoor's team version reads: 3 hours of wrong prices on 1,140 products; 2,310 orders placed; 412 orders refunded the difference. All three figures are constructed. Notice what each one does: the hours give the duration, the products the blast radius, and the refunds the money and the customer cost, which is what the public version in Leader session 3 will have to explain.

Also say what the incident was not. If checkout stayed up, write that; calling a pricing error an outage makes the next real outage harder to rank.

Real world

Work forward to beat hindsight. PagerDuty's public postmortem guide, on its blameless page, draws on Dekker's old view and new view of human error, where error is a symptom of a problem in the system, and gives a practical counter to hindsight bias: begin the timeline before the incident and move forward through it, rather than back from the outcome. (PagerDuty, Postmortem guide, docs.pagerduty.com, read at source 2026-11-11.)

Part 3 · covers conditions and actions

Conditions you can change, actions someone owns

5 min live

The "why" section names the conditions that made the incident possible, usually several, rather than one root cause and one person. The actions section turns each condition into a change: one owner, one date, specific enough that everyone agrees when it is done. A line with no owner and no date is a wish, and wishes do not get done.

LiveConditions, and the root cause field3 min▶

Write conditions, plural. PM-11's team version names three: the validator compared columns and never values; no alert watched for a price jump against the last feed; the runbook for feed rejection had no step for a feed that passes. Each one is a thing the team can change. None of them is "Priya", and none of them is "should have".

Why "should have" is flagged. Dekker's field guide (reported) argues that counterfactuals describe a world that did not happen. "The validator should have caught it" tells you nothing about why it did not; "the validator checks the schema, not the values" does. Write what people saw and why their action made sense at the time.

The disagreement. PagerDuty and Dekker favour contributing conditions over a single root cause, and many templates still carry a root-cause field; the SRE book's own definition names root causes. Both are in use. If your template has the field, fill it with the condition you would fix first and list the others beside it, so the field cannot become a person. What a postmortem may conclude is argued in full in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗.

Self-studyWhat turns a wish into an action4 min▶

One owner. The SRE workbook's good postmortem gives every action a single owner with collaborators, a priority, a tracking bug and a type: investigate, prevent, mitigate, repair or detect. Many owners is one of the faults it lists in the bad version.

A date. Without a due date there is nothing to chase and nothing to be late against. Writer session 5 shows what open actions with no date did to Pellmoor's year.

Specific and bounded. PagerDuty asks for actions that someone can act on, that say exactly what changes, and whose scope has an end. "Make validation better" has no end; "add an outlier check against the last accepted feed" does.

Change the system before the person. The workbook notes that changing human behaviour is less reliable than changing automated systems. "Be more careful with supplier files" relies on the most tired person on the rota noticing a cents-for-pounds file. A page on more than 50 large price changes in one file does not get tired.

Real world

Look for the condition, not the culprit. PagerDuty's guide on effective postmortems observes that the mistake is very rarely rooted in a person; it sits in the system, the tooling or the process that let a reasonable action do harm. Its blameless page builds on Dekker and on Allspaw's advice to ask how something happened rather than why someone did it. (PagerDuty, Postmortem guide, docs.pagerduty.com, read at source 2026-11-11; Dekker and Allspaw, reported.)

Real world

Constructed Pellmoor example: PM-11 is a repeat. In Pellmoor's corpus, "unvalidated supplier input" is tagged on two postmortems, PM-04 and PM-11, and the bench traces PM-11 back to PM-04's action PM-04.1, still open when the cents-for-pounds file arrived. Writer session 5 reads the eleven postmortems together to find it. Tonight's point is smaller: an action that is not finished is a condition for the next incident.

Build-along

Lint PM-11, then write it properly

★ 22 min · everyone builds

The widget is the linter, loaded with the first draft of PM-11. You lint it, fix the timeline and the actions yourself in the text box, compare with Pellmoor's team version, then write your own skeleton. Copy what the widget prints. PM-11 is constructed; the rule engine is real. It checks shape, not truth: a draft can score 100 and still name the wrong conditions.

Homework

Before Writer session 5

30 min
  1. Write your team's triggers. In five lines or fewer, list which incidents owe a postmortem and by when, using the SRE book's triggers as the starting list.
  2. Lint one of your own postmortems. Paste it into the linter, record the summary line, and note which rules fail most.
  3. Rewrite its timeline with absolute dates and UTC, starting before anything looked wrong, with no person as the subject of a line.
  4. Audit its actions. For each one, mark owner, date, and whether it changes a system or asks a person to change. Count the wishes.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does a postmortem timeline use UTC and absolute dates rather than "this morning around 9am"?

"This morning" is true for one day and "09:40" for one zone. The postmortem is read at the review, in the monthly reading and when the same feed fails next year.

2 · The draft says "The validator should have caught it." Why does the linter flag "should have"?

A counterfactual tells you what you wish had happened. "The validator checks the schema, not the values" tells you what to change.

3 · Which change turns "Be more careful with supplier files" from a wish into an action?

An action has one owner, one date and a bounded scope, and changing an automated system beats asking a tired person to notice.
