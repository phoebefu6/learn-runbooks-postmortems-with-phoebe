Part 1 · covers why actions are the point

A postmortem with no follow-through

6 min live

The timeline, the impact numbers and the contributing conditions are all in service of one thing: something changes so the same incident does not come back. An action item is the only part of a postmortem that touches production. If it never closes, the rest was a well-written history lesson.

LiveThe line every follow-through program hangs on2 min▶

Google's SRE workbook, in its chapter on postmortem culture, quotes Ben Treynor Sloss: a postmortem "without subsequent action is indistinguishable from no postmortem" (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10). It is the bluntest statement of why the action list matters more than the prose. A leader cannot read every postmortem. A leader can ask, every month, how many of the actions those postmortems promised are really done.

LiveOne owner, the team's tracker, a reward for closing2 min▶

The same chapter describes how Google keeps actions alive. Action items are filed as bugs in a central tracker, so their closure can be monitored like any other work. People are rewarded for closing actions, not just for writing them. And twice a year there are FixIt weeks, time set aside to clear the backlog.

The workbook's good example postmortem gives each action a type, a priority, a tracking bug and one owner, and states the preference plainly: better a single owner and several collaborators than a list of owners. Four owners on one action means each of the four can assume one of the others is on it.

Self-studyFive reasons actions fail, a vendor's list3 min▶

incident.io, a vendor of incident tooling, published a blog post in 2026 on why postmortem action items fail. It names five reasons: no individual owner; actions kept in the postmortem document instead of the team's own tracker; vague wording; no follow-up cadence; and systemic problems framed as technical tasks. The post gives no measured rates, so treat it as a practitioner's checklist, not evidence of how often each one happens.

The fifth reason is the one that reaches a leader's desk. "Add a validation step" is a technical task an engineer can close. "Suppliers can push prices with no contract on format" is a systemic problem, and the person who can fix it may sit in procurement, not engineering. Framing it as a ticket guarantees it stays open, or closes with the real problem untouched.

Real world

Tracked like any other bug. Google files postmortem action items as bugs in a central tracker so closure can be monitored, rewards the people who close them, and runs FixIt weeks twice a year to clear what is left. The design choice that matters is the first one: the action lives where the team already plans its work, not in a document nobody reopens. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the two numbers that tell the truth

Closure rate, median age, and really done

5 min live

Two numbers summarise a year of follow-through. The closure rate says how many promised actions have closed. The median age of open actions says how long the ones still open have been waiting; a closure rate can look respectable while the open ones rot for half a year. Beside both sits a third counter, really done, which counts only the actions whose work was finished. Tonight you will see why it has to be there.

LiveReading R4 against R52 min▶

At R4, Pellmoor has done everything the Writer track asked of its runbooks: inventory, owners, linter-clean rewrites, change-triggered review and a drill rota. The corpus has not moved at all. The bench prints "Actions closed: 13 of 46 (28%) · really done: 13", a median age of open actions of 24 weeks, and 2 of 2 repeat incidents traceable to an open action. Owners and dates are necessary; on their own they do not close anything.

R5 adds one practice, an action chase every 2 weeks. Closed goes to 44 of 46 (96%), really done to 44, the median age of open actions to 5 weeks, and the repeats to 0 of 2. The two still open belong to PM-11, the last postmortem of the year in week 47, too recent to finish. That is a healthy reason for an action to be open; the median age tells you whether the open ones are young or forgotten. Constructed Pellmoor example.

LiveWhy chasing a wish does not close it2 min▶

Learner move m3 on the bench gives every action an owner and chases every week, but skips the linter-clean rewrites that turn wishes into an owner, a date and a clear definition of done. Closed rises to 21 of 46 (46%). Repeats stay at 2 of 2. The owner of "improve monitoring" can be asked about it every Monday for a year and still have no way to say it is finished, because nothing on the page says what finished looks like. With owners alone (R1), the bench still prints 18 of 46 actions written as wishes; a chase cadence cannot reach any of them.

The order matters for a leader: fix the wording first, then chase. A cadence applied to wishes produces meetings, not closures.

Real world

The repeat is the signal. Among the warning signs of a failing postmortem culture, the SRE workbook lists incidents that repeat. A repeat traced to an action that was written, agreed and left open is the clearest version of that signal a leader will ever get, which is why it belongs on the report as its own line rather than buried in a closure rate. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers the Goodhart trap and the report upward

A dashboard that rewards closing tickets

4 min live

Once closure rate goes on a dashboard, people will make it go up, and the cheapest way to make it go up is to close things. A measure that becomes a target stops measuring what it used to; economists call this Goodhart's law. The fix is not a better closure rate. It is a second number beside it that cannot be raised by closing tickets.

LiveThe auto-close trap, T22 min▶

Trap T2 on the bench marks every action closed when its due date passes, without the work. It is not a strawman: ticket tools offer it, and a team measured on closure rate will find it. The bench prints "Actions closed: 46 of 46 (100%) · really done: 13". The median age of open actions drops to 0 weeks, because nothing is open any more. Repeats stay at 2 of 2. On a dashboard that shows only closure, T2 is the best-run year Pellmoor has ever had. Constructed Pellmoor example.

Really done is the counter that does not lie, because it cannot be moved by changing a ticket's status. It moves only when the work is finished. Put it in the column next to closed, every time.

LiveWhat the report must show2 min▶

Five lines, one page. Closed and really done side by side, so the gap is visible. The median age of open actions, so slow rot shows even when closure looks fine. The actions with no owner or no date, counted as wishes, so the cause of a stuck list is named. The repeat incidents traced to an open action, so the cost is in the same place as the backlog. And the one systemic action that needs a leader's authority, a budget, a contract or another team's priority, named with the decision you are asking for.

The last line is the reason the report goes upward at all. Engineering can close its own tickets. It cannot change a supplier contract.

Self-studyTrends across the year, not one quarter2 min▶

A single report says where you stand. A run of them says whether the program works. Writer session 5 reads Pellmoor's eleven postmortems as one dataset, tagging the conditions behind each; the follow-through report is the natural place to carry the result forward: which conditions keep coming back, and whether the actions aimed at them are the ones that are really done. The danluu index of public postmortems, grouped by kind of failure, is a reminder that the same conditions repeat across companies too: danluu/post-mortems ↗.

Real world

A repository, read for trends. The SRE workbook describes a searchable repository of thousands of postmortems going back to 2009, analysed for trends such as postmortems per month, time to detect and resolve, and blast radius. Follow-through belongs in that same habit: a number tracked over time, not a status checked once at the review. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Homework

After Leader session 4

30 min
  1. Count your two numbers. From your last year of postmortems, count actions closed and the median age of the ones still open.
  2. Sample ten closed actions and check how many were really done, not closed by a date, a duplicate or a reorg.
  3. Rewrite your three oldest wishes as one owner, one date and one sentence saying what done looks like, before you chase anyone.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On the bench, auto-closing actions at their due date (T2) prints 46 of 46 closed. Why is that not good news?

Closed counts a status change. Really done counts finished work, and it does not move when tickets are closed by a rule.

2 · With owners and a weekly chase but no rewrites (m3), closed rises to 21 of 46 and repeats stay at 2 of 2. Why?

Fix the wording first, then chase. A cadence applied to "improve monitoring" produces meetings, not closures.

3 · Which set of lines belongs on the follow-through report a leader takes upward?

One page, five lines; the last one asks for a decision only a leader can make.
