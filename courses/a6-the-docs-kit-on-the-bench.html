Part 1 · covers the library ladder

Which practice moves which number

4 min live

Each rung adds one practice to the one before. The big fall is not where most teams expect it. Owners and rewrites help; the drop from 41% to 14% comes when every system change sends the touched pages to their owners, because staleness tracks change. Drills then catch what review missed.

LiveWhy the rungs fall where they do3 min▶

R1, owners. Every page gets an owner who reads it once, and the six missing pages get written. A read of a page whose commands have no expected output catches a wrong step 40% of the time in the model; most survive.

R2, rewrites. Owned pages pass the linter, so a read now catches a wrong step 90% of the time, and wishes in the corpus become actions with owners and dates. The share falls to 41%, but the quarter's changes keep landing on pages nobody re-reads.

R3, change-triggered review. A change that touches a page sends it to its owner. Staleness tracks change, so this is the rung that meets the drift where it happens: 6 of 44.

R4, drills. A drill catches every wrong step on the page it runs. A rota of once a quarter for every paged page leaves 2 of 44, both unpaged pages that no drill covers.

Self-studyThe model's rates, all on the widget3 min▶

Drift per change: 30% by default, simulated, movable with the slider. Catch rate when an owner reads or reviews a page: 90% if the page is linter-clean, 40% if not; also simulated, shipped as fixed rolls so every browser prints the same numbers. A drill catches 100%. No public measurement of these rates exists that the bench could borrow, so the bench claims only their ordering: review on change beats a one-off read, a clean page is easier to check than a vague one, and a drill is the only full test.

Real world

Update the page when it fires. The SRE workbook asks on-call engineers to update the playbook with fresh information when its page fires, and notes that playbook details go out of date as fast as production changes. R3 on the bench is that habit made into a rule: the change, not the calendar, triggers the review. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, "On-Call", read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the two traps

Two buttons that only look like progress

4 min live

A bench that can only report better numbers measures nothing. Two trap buttons report a better headline and a worse reality. T1 deletes stale pages: fewer misleading pages, more alerts with nothing behind them. T2 marks actions closed at their due date: closure looks perfect, and both repeat incidents still happen.

LiveT2 is the one that ships in real dashboards3 min▶

Closure rate is easy to count and easy to game: close the ticket when the date passes, or reword the action until it is "done". The SRE workbook quotes Ben Treynor Sloss: a postmortem without subsequent action is indistinguishable from no postmortem. Marking actions closed is not subsequent action. The bench keeps two counters for this reason: closed, and really done. Leader session 4 is about the report that shows both.

Self-studyT1 is a real position, not a strawman2 min▶

Deleting stale pages has serious defenders: a stale page is trusted, and trust in a wrong step costs more than a blank. Leader session 1 teaches both camps. On the bench the trade is visible: 16 pages deleted, the share falls from 66% to 45%, and 14 of 31 alerts now page a person with nothing behind them. Whether that trade is right depends on how your responders behave on a blank page, which only a drill can tell you.

Part 3 · covers the corpus

Actions are the only rung that stops a repeat

4 min live

The library rungs make pages true; they do nothing for the corpus. Through R4, Pellmoor still closes 13 of 46 actions and both repeat incidents still happen. Only the chase, an owner and a date checked every two weeks, closes the two actions whose absence let PM-09 and PM-11 happen.

LiveWhat R5 changes, line by line3 min▶

At R4: actions closed 13 of 46 (28%), median age of open actions 24 weeks, repeat incidents traceable to an open action 2 of 2. At R5: 44 of 46 (96%), 5 weeks, 0 of 2. The two still open belong to the last postmortem of the year, too recent to finish. R2 had already turned every wish into an owner and a date; nothing closed until somebody checked.

Self-studyWhy vendors and Google agree on this one2 min▶

Google files postmortem actions as bugs in a central tracker so it can monitor closure, and rewards closing them, not just writing them. A 2026 incident.io post lists five reasons actions fail: no individual owner, kept in the document instead of the team's tracker, vague wording, no follow-up cadence, and systemic problems framed as technical tasks. It gives no measured rates, so quote none. (SRE workbook ch. 10, read at source; incident.io blog, vendor view, read at source.)

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · On Pellmoor's ladder, which rung gives the largest single fall in the share of pages that would mislead?

Staleness tracks change. Review triggered by the change meets drift where it happens, on a page that is now easy to check.

2 · T2 marks every action closed at its due date. What does the bench print that gives it away?

Marking an action closed is not doing it. The bench keeps closed and really done as separate counters for exactly this reason.

3 · The rewritten RB-07 scores 100 on the linter. What can you still not say about it?

The linter checks shape, not truth. A clean page is easy to check; only a drill checks it.
