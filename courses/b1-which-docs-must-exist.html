Which docs must exist: a standard, not a wish list

"We should document more" is a wish. A docs standard is three rules you can check against the library on any Monday: every alert that can wake a person links to a page; every incident above a severity line gets a postmortem, by a deadline; every page and every postmortem has one named owner. Tonight you write Pellmoor's standard, check it on the bench, and face the argument every docs owner meets sooner or later: is a stale page worse than no page at all?

Part 1 · covers the docs standard

Three rules you can check on a Monday

6 min live

A standard earns the name when a person who was not in the room can check it. Each of the three rules below can be checked with a query or a count, which is why they survive. "Keep documentation up to date" cannot, which is why it never does.

LiveRule 1 · every paging alert has a page2 min▶

If an alert can wake a person, it links to a page. Google's SRE workbook states the norm as each alert having a corresponding playbook entry, created with the alert. The check is a join between the paging configuration and the wiki; Writer session 1 runs it. The count of alerts with no page goes on the leader's dashboard and should read zero.

LiveRule 2 · a severity line for postmortems, with deadlines2 min▶

Above the line, a postmortem is required; below it, optional. The SRE book lists the kinds of event that should trigger one: user-visible downtime or degradation past a threshold, data loss of any kind, an on-call intervention such as a rollback, a resolution time past a threshold, and a monitoring failure, which usually means a person found the incident by hand.

PagerDuty publishes its own internal policy: a postmortem for every major incident (Sev-1 and Sev-2), completed within 3 calendar days for a Sev-1 and 5 business days for a Sev-2. Those are one company's numbers, not a standard; the point is that a deadline exists and is written down.

LiveRule 3 · one owner, not a team2 min▶

PagerDuty's guide has the incident commander pick one responder to own the postmortem, so nobody assumes somebody else has it; the writing is still collaborative. The SRE workbook makes the same call for action items: better a single owner and several collaborators than four owners. The rule applies to pages too: one named role per page, recorded at the top of the page.

Real world

A deadline is part of the standard. The SRE workbook's worked example sets a bad postmortem, published four months after the outage, beside a good one, written and circulated less than a week after the incident closed, and notes that prompt postmortems tend to be more accurate. A standard with no deadline produces the first kind. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the stale page argument

Is a stale page worse than none?

5 min live

Sooner or later someone proposes the clean-up: delete every page nobody has verified in a year. It is a real position, held by experienced people, and it is not settled. The bench can show you what it buys and what it costs on Pellmoor's library, so the argument happens on numbers.

LiveBoth camps, in their own terms3 min▶

Delete it. A stale page is trusted. A responder follows a wrong step with confidence, at the worst hour, and the page made the incident longer. No page at least makes the responder think and escalate.

Keep it, flagged. A partly stale page still says which dashboard, which team, what failed last time. A blank page gives the responder nothing. Better to mark it unverified at the top and route it to an owner.

The SRE workbook records a related split without settling it: some teams keep entries general so they change slowly, others want step-by-step instructions, and it asks each team to agree the minimal structure its pages must have. Your standard should state which camp it is in, and why, in one sentence.

Self-studyA third answer: a banner instead of a bin2 min▶

Some teams split the difference: a page past its verification window shows a banner ("not verified since ...; steps may be wrong; escalate if a step fails") and is routed to its owner. The banner keeps the context and removes the false confidence. The bench does not model the banner; whether it works depends on whether the 2 a.m. reader reads it, which is a question for a drill (Writer session 3).

Real world

The old view and the new view. PagerDuty's guide to blameless postmortems cites Sidney Dekker's distinction between an old view of human error and a new view that treats it as a symptom of a systemic problem. A responder misled by a stale page is the textbook case: the error is in the library, not in the person. (PagerDuty postmortem guide, read at source; Dekker's book, reported.)

Part 3 · covers what the standard is checked against

A standard you can read off the bench

4 min live

A leader does not read 38 runbooks. A leader reads four numbers every month: alerts with no page, pages with no owner, the share of pages that would mislead, and postmortems overdue against their deadline. The first three come straight off the Writer track's bench; the fourth comes from your incident tracker.

LiveRules versus outcome2 min▶

Inventory and owners (R1 on the bench) meet rule 1 and rule 3 completely on Pellmoor: alerts with no page go from 6 to 0 of 31, pages with no owner from 15 to 0. The outcome moves less: 23 of 44 pages still mislead (52%). That gap is the honest message to take upward. A standard buys accountability; it does not by itself make pages true. Writer sessions 2 and 3 do that with rewrites, change-triggered review and drills.

Self-studySecurity incidents and regulated docs2 min▶

Security incidents carry docs obligations of their own, often with external clocks. The first hour of a security incident, and what must be written down during it, is taught in Cyber Security, the first hour ↗. Your docs standard should point at that playbook rather than duplicate it.

Publish the standard where the pages live. A standard filed in a policy folder is read once. A standard linked from the top of the runbook index, with this month's four numbers beside it, is read every time someone looks for a page.

Homework

Before Leader session 2

30 min
  1. Draw your severity line. List your last ten incidents with their severity and mark which ones got a postmortem, and how many days later.
  2. Ask for the join. Ask whoever owns paging for the count of alerts that can wake a person and link to no page.
  3. Name one owner for the five pages behind your noisiest alerts, and put the name on each page.
  4. Write your stale-page sentence and show it to the person on your team most likely to disagree.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which of these is a docs standard rather than a wish?

A standard is checkable by someone who was not in the room: a rule, a check and a target.

2 · On the bench, deleting every page not verified in a year cuts the share that misleads from 66% to 45%. What does the same run also print?

The clean-up trades misleading pages for blank ones. Whether that trade is right is the open argument; the bench makes both sides visible.

3 · Why does the postmortem owner have to be one person rather than the team?

PagerDuty names one owner to avoid the bystander effect; the writing stays collaborative, and the owner is not the person blamed.
