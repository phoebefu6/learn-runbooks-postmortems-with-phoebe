Part 1 · covers the inventory

Four fields every page owes the library

5 min live

A runbook on its own is a document. A runbook in a library is a promise: someone owns it, someone checked it against the system on a known date, a known alert sends people to it, and it has been tried by a person who did not write it. Four fields carry that promise. A page missing any of them is a page nobody can vouch for.

LiveThe four fields, and why each one3 min▶

Owner. A named role, not a team and not "everyone". When a system change lands, somebody has to be the person the change is routed to. A page with no owner has nobody to route to, so it silently ages.

Last verified. The date someone walked the steps against the live system and found them true. Not the date the page was last edited: an edit to the wording is not a check of the commands.

Alert bound. Which paging alert links to this page. Google's SRE workbook puts the norm plainly: each alert should have a corresponding playbook entry. Binding works both ways. An alert with no page sends a responder into the dark; a page bound to no alert is only found by search, usually by the wrong person.

Last drilled. When someone who did not write the page last ran it, timed, in a drill or a game day. It is the only field that tests the page rather than describing it, which is why Writer session 3 is about nothing else.

Self-studyOne page, recapped in one card (and where to learn it properly)3 min▶

This track does not reteach how to write a single good runbook; two sibling sessions already do. In short: a runbook is a how-to guide in the Diataxis sense, written for a competent reader with one goal, as a sequence of actions with no digression or teaching; every action is followed by a way to confirm it worked; and it is written for a reader who is tired, new to the system and reading on a phone.

For the anatomy, read Tech Writing, runbooks ↗. For one runbook bound to one alert on a data pipeline, read Data Reliability, runbooks ↗. Sentence craft lives in Tech Writing 02 ↗. Here, the unit is the library.

Real world

Google pairs the alert and the page at birth. The SRE workbook's on-call chapter describes the convention that when an alert is created, a corresponding playbook entry usually is too, and that pages should link to the relevant monitoring consoles. The inventory you build tonight is how you find out whether your team kept that convention, alert by alert. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, "On-Call", read at source.)

Part 2 · covers the four states

True, stale, orphaned, missing

5 min live

Once every page has its four fields, every page falls into one of four states. True: the steps match the system and somebody owns it. Stale: a step no longer matches the system. Orphaned: nobody owns it, so nobody will notice when it goes stale. Missing: an alert pages a human and there is no page at all. Stale is the dangerous one, because a stale page is still trusted.

LiveCount from both ends4 min▶

Walk the wiki and you find pages: true, stale, orphaned. You never find the missing ones, because they do not exist. So the inventory runs from two ends. From the paging configuration, list every alert that can wake a human and write the page it links to, or "none". From the wiki, list every page and write the alert that sends people to it, or "none".

The join gives you three groups: alerts with a page, alerts with no page (missing), and pages no alert reaches. The last group is not automatically junk; a month-end close checklist is not paged. But it is found only by search, and a page found by search is read by whoever searched, not by the person on call.

Self-studyIs a stale page worse than no page?3 min▶

Practitioners disagree, and the disagreement is worth knowing. One camp says a stale page is worse than none, because the reader trusts it and follows a wrong step with confidence. The other says even a partly stale page carries context (which dashboard, which team, what failed last time) that a blank cannot. The SRE workbook records a related split: some teams keep entries general so they change slowly, others insist on step-by-step instructions. The bench has a trap button for the first camp's answer, deleting the stale pages; you will see what it costs in Writer session 6.

Real world

Staleness tracks change, not age. The SRE workbook's on-call chapter says details in playbooks go out of date at the same rate as production changes, and that on-call engineers should update the playbook when its page fires. A page about a system nobody has touched for a year may still be true; a page about a system that ships daily can be wrong by Friday. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, "On-Call", read at source.)

Part 3 · covers the baseline

One number for the whole library

5 min live

An inventory ends in one number a leader can track: of every page, how many would mislead the next responder tonight? A page misleads if any step in it is wrong. On Pellmoor's constructed library the bench answers 25 of 38. That is the baseline every later session works on.

LiveHow the bench decides a page would mislead3 min▶

The library model is small and stated on the widget. Each of 36 system changes in the quarter touches one to three pages of its system. Each touch breaks a step with a probability you can move, the drift rate, set to 30% by default. That rate is simulated; nobody has published a measured rate you could borrow, and yours depends on how often your systems change.

Older pages also start the quarter carrying wrong steps: the less recently verified, the more. A page misleads if it ends the quarter with any wrong step. Owners, reviews and drills catch wrong steps at stated rates. Later sessions turn each of those on, one at a time.

Self-studyWhy write pages at all: the reported 3x2 min▶

The SRE book's introduction says recording best practice ahead of time in a playbook produces roughly a 3x improvement in mean time to repair, compared with "winging it". Treat it as Google's reported experience, not a measured study: the book gives no method. It is still the right shape of claim for a library: the gain comes from pages that are true when read. A stale page takes the reader confidently in the wrong direction.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Writer session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Your wiki lists 40 runbooks and every one has an owner. Which state can that inventory still not see?

A walk of the wiki only finds pages that exist. Start from the paging configuration to find alerts with nothing behind them.

2 · Which field is the only one that tests the page rather than describing it?

An owner, a date and a link describe the page. A drill runs it against the system with a reader who brings no memory of it.

3 · On the bench, R1 gives every page an owner and writes the six missing pages. Why does the share that misleads fall only from 66% to 52%?

Ownership is necessary, not sufficient. A one-off read misses wrong steps, and the quarter's changes keep arriving after it.
