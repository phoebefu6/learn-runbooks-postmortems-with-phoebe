The review that people come to: read first, then talk

A postmortem nobody reviews changes nothing. A review people skip is not much better. Tonight you design the review that works: the document goes out a day ahead, the meeting has three agenda items and ends with decisions written down, and the facilitator keeps the room on how the system let it happen, not on who. Then you widen the habit beyond your own incidents, with a reading club built on old, public write-ups. Pellmoor's pricing postmortem is the one you rehearse on.

Part 1 · covers the review ritual

The document goes first, the meeting follows

5 min live

Google's SRE book lists leaving no postmortem unreviewed among its best practices, and puts the reason bluntly: "An unreviewed postmortem might as well never have existed." (Google, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15, read at source.) The review is where a draft becomes a set of commitments. PagerDuty's published guide shows one way to run it, and the order matters more than the room: the written postmortem comes first, the meeting follows it.

LiveThe 24 hours and the three agenda items2 min▶

In PagerDuty's guide, the owner sends the document to attendees 24 hours before the meeting, finished or not. A meeting that starts with people reading spends its best minutes catching up; a meeting that starts with people who have read can spend them deciding.

The agenda then runs in three items. The owner summarises the causes and the timeline. The room discusses the action items: will they reduce the chance of a repeat, and will leadership commit to them? Then, for the incidents that touched customers, the customer liaison summarises the customer impact and the room reviews the external message. The liaison attends for a Sev-1 and is optional otherwise. (PagerDuty, Postmortem guide, read at source.)

Before anyone leaves, two things are written down: the actions accepted, each with one owner and a date, and the external message approved or sent back. Leader session 3 is about that message; Leader session 4 is about making the actions close.

LiveWho reads what, before the room2 min▶

"Send it ahead" does not mean everyone reads all of it. Say in the invite which part each reader owns:

  • The team that responded reads the whole document and checks the timeline against what they remember and what the logs show.
  • Executives read the summary and the actions. Their job in the room is the second agenda question: will we commit to these?
  • Support and the customer liaison read the impact section, because they will be asked about it by customers before anyone else is.

Naming the reading per role is a small courtesy that makes the 24 hours work. A busy executive who knows the two sections to read will read them.

Self-studyTimeboxing and attendance3 min▶

PagerDuty's guide suggests you can timebox each agenda item, so a long timeline discussion does not eat the action review. It also lists who attends, and keeps its review meetings on a company-wide calendar that anyone may join; completed postmortems go to a shared Incident Reports list. Open meetings and a shared list are what turn one team's review into the organisation's learning.

The deadline for holding the review is your standard's business, set in Leader session 1. A useful check for the ritual: the review happens before the postmortem's own deadline, not after, so the actions it accepts are in the document that gets published. (PagerDuty, Postmortem guide, read at source.)

Real world

Late reviews read differently. The SRE workbook's worked example sets a bad postmortem, published four months after the outage to a limited audience, beside a good one written and circulated less than a week after the incident closed. The review is part of that clock: a draft waiting for a meeting slot is a draft nobody acts on. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers blameless facilitation in the room

The facilitator asks how, not who

5 min live

A blameless document can still meet a blameful room. The facilitator is the person who keeps the meeting on the system: in PagerDuty's guide, the facilitator talks as little as they can, takes no sides, and keeps the agenda moving. What a postmortem may conclude, and why blameless is the entry condition, is taught in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗. Here it is about the three moves a facilitator makes out loud.

LiveBlameless, said out loud2 min▶

The SRE book's blameless postmortem focuses on contributing causes and assumes everyone involved had good intentions. In the room that becomes a habit of grammar. When a sentence has a person's name as the subject of a mistake ("the analyst pushed the feed without looking"), the facilitator restates it with the role and the condition: the pricing on-call pushed a feed, and the tooling gave them no diff to look at. The person is still in the story. The question has moved to what the system let happen.

PagerDuty's blameless page also points to John Allspaw's essay "The Infinite Hows": ask how something happened, not why someone did it. "Why did you push it?" asks for a defence. "How did the feed get accepted?" asks for a mechanism. (PagerDuty guide, read at source; Allspaw's essay cited there, reported.)

LiveHindsight, and the "should have"2 min▶

Everyone in the review knows how the incident ended. The people in the timeline did not. PagerDuty's answer to hindsight bias is procedural: start the timeline before the incident and work forward, so the room sees what each person saw at each moment. "Should have" describes a world that did not happen (Dekker, reported); the facilitator's reply is a question about the world that did: what did you see at 09:40, and what did the screen show you?

Self-studyWhat the facilitator does not do2 min▶

The facilitator does not argue the root cause, does not grade the draft, and does not take the side of the team or of leadership. Whether a postmortem should name one root cause or a set of contributing conditions is a live disagreement between practitioners and templates; it is argued in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗, not here. The facilitator's job is narrower: keep the room on the agenda, on the system, and on decisions that get written down.

Real world

The warning sign at the top. The SRE workbook's postmortem chapter lists warning signs of a failing postmortem culture, and two of them belong to the review room: incidents that repeat, and leaders using blameful language. A blameless template does not survive a vice president asking "who did this?" in the meeting. That is why the facilitator's reframe matters most when the senior person in the room is the one speaking. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers learning beyond your own incidents

A reading club for incidents that are not yours

5 min live

Your own incidents arrive at their own pace, and a quiet quarter teaches nothing. The SRE book describes rituals that keep the learning going between them: a postmortem of the month, sent out as a newsletter; reading clubs that discuss incidents months or years old; and the Wheel of Misfortune, where a past postmortem is re-enacted with engineers playing the roles. (Google, Site Reliability Engineering, ch. 15, read at source.) The SRE workbook adds cross-team reviews and weekly outage reports to the same family.

LiveWhy old incidents teach well2 min▶

A reading club on an incident months or years old has advantages your own review cannot have. Nobody in the room was on call for it, so nobody is defending a decision. The outcome is known, so the discussion can go straight to the conditions. And the question the club asks is about your system, not theirs: could this happen to us, and which of our pages would we open?

LiveA reading club built on a public index2 min▶

Dan Luu's post-mortems index on GitHub ↗ collects links to public write-ups and groups them under Config Errors, Hardware/Power Failures, Conflicts, Time, Database and Uncategorized, with other lists and analysis after them. Config errors is the largest named group, at about 60 entries. The grouping is what makes it useful for a club: pick a category that matches a condition your own corpus keeps tagging, pick one entry, and read the original write-up it links to.

A simple session shape: one entry per session, chosen by category; everyone reads it the day before, the same courtesy as your own reviews; and the discussion answers two questions. Could this happen to us? Which of our pages would we open, and would they help? The index states no licence, so link to it and to the write-ups; do not copy their summaries into your own materials.

LiveConstructed Pellmoor example: the first reading club2 min▶

Constructed Pellmoor example. After the pricing-feed incident, the head of engineering starts a monthly reading club: six people, thirty minutes, the last Thursday of the month. The first session picks from the Config Errors group, because Pellmoor's own corpus already shows a bad input accepted by a check that looked only at the shape of the file. The entry is sent out the day before.

The discussion takes the two questions in order. Could this happen to us? The warehouse picking lead says yes, and names the supplier slot file as the same kind of input. Which of our pages would we open? Nobody can name one. That silence becomes the club's only output: an action to write the page, with one owner role and a date, logged in the same tracker as every postmortem action. (Every detail here is constructed.)

Self-studyWheel of Misfortune and the drill2 min▶

A Wheel of Misfortune session re-enacts a past postmortem, with one engineer playing the responder and another playing the system. It overlaps with the runbook drill taught in Writer session 3, with a different aim: a drill tests the page, the wheel tests the responder's reasoning. Run one from your own reviewed postmortems once the reading club is steady.

Real world

Learning from thousands, not one. The SRE workbook describes a searchable repository of thousands of postmortems going back to 2009, analysed for trends such as postmortems per month, time to detect and resolve, and blast radius. A reading club is the small, human version of the same idea: the corpus is bigger than your own incidents, and reading it on purpose is how a team gets ahead of the next one. (Google, The Site Reliability Workbook, ch. 10, read at source.)

Build-along

The review rehearsal

★ 22 min · everyone works it through

The widget is the linter the Writer track uses, loaded with the first draft of Pellmoor's PM-11, the pricing-feed postmortem. You will not rewrite it. You will read its findings as a facilitator would, write the question you would ask in the room for each, then write the invite and the ritual. The rule engine is real and runs on any text you paste; PM-11 is constructed. The linter checks shape, not truth: a draft can score 100 and still name the wrong conditions.

Step 1 · Lint the first draft. The widget opens on the PM-11 first draft. Read the summary line and the findings list, rule by rule.

Step 2 · Write the facilitator's question for each failing rule. One question per rule, phrased for the room, not as an edit to the document. The first two are filled in as a pattern.

★ Step 2 · one question per ruleowner (line 1): Who owns this postmortem until its actions are done? time (lines 5, 6, 7): Can we put each event in UTC, with the date, from the logs? blame (lines 6, 13): ______________________________________________ counterfactual (line 13): ______________________________________________ wish (lines 16, 17, 18): ______________________________________________ impact (line 9): ______________________________________________

Step 3 · Load the team version. Switch the sample to the PM-11 team version and copy the summary line. Then write one sentence on what that score cannot tell the room.

Step 5 · Write Pellmoor's review ritual. Then copy it and fill it for your own team.

★ Step 5 · review ritualWho: owner, responding team, a facilitator who was not on call, leadership for actions, customer liaison for Sev-1 When: review held within ____ of the incident closing, and before the postmortem deadline in our docs standard Before: document out 24 hours ahead, reading named per role Recorded: actions with one owner and a date; external message decision; postmortem filed in our shared incident reports list Facilitator moves: blame -> role + condition; should have -> what did you see; vague action -> who, by when Reading club: every ____ ; one entry from the public index by category; read the day before; any action it produces goes in the tracker

Rotate the facilitator. If the same person facilitates every review, the role becomes a job title and then a bottleneck. Rotate it among people who were not on call for the incident, and give each new facilitator the three moves on a card.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does the postmortem go to attendees 24 hours before the review, even if it is unfinished?

The written postmortem comes first and the meeting follows it. Time spent catching up in the room is time not spent on the actions and the external message.

2 · In the room, someone says "the analyst should have checked the outliers." What does the facilitator do?

"Should have" describes a world that did not happen. The facilitator takes no sides and turns the sentence into a question about what the system showed at the time.

3 · What does a reading club gain from discussing an incident months or years old, from another organisation?

The SRE book's reading clubs work on old incidents. Distance removes the defensiveness; the useful question is about your own system and your own pages.
