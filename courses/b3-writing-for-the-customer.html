Writing for the customer: one incident, three readers

The review approved an external message, or sent it back. Tonight you write it. The same incident goes to three readers who want different things: the team wants everything, the executive wants the impact, the cost and the decision, and the customer wants to know what happened to them, what you are doing about it and how to reach you. You cut all three from one team postmortem, learn what to redact and what to leave close to as-is, and face the argument nobody has settled: apologise first, or explain first?

Part 1 · covers three readers of one incident

Same facts, three documents

6 min live

One incident does not get one write-up. It gets as many as it has readers, and each reader needs a different cut of the same facts. Writing one document for all three produces a page the team finds thin, the executive finds long and the customer finds full of names they do not know.

LiveThe team version · everything2 min▶

The team version is the base all others are cut from: a UTC timeline, impact as numbers, the contributing conditions, and actions with one owner and one date each. Writer session 4 builds it from the incident record, and the linter checks its shape. It names internal systems, runbooks and roles, because its readers fix those things. Its rules for what a postmortem may conclude are taught in Data Reliability, postmortems ↗; not repeated here.

LiveThe executive version · impact, cost, decision2 min▶

The executive reads five lines and acts on one of them. Impact and cost come first, in numbers. The why is one sentence with no system names. Then the decision you need from them, stated as a question they can answer yes or no. Then the actions with owners and dates, and when they will hear next. If there is no decision needed, say so: that is also information. The most common failure is an executive version that is the team version with the timeline deleted, which buries the one line they needed.

LiveThe customer note · their terms, not yours2 min▶

The customer did not see a validator, a feed or a schema. They saw a wrong price, maybe paid it, maybe got a refund. The note tells them what happened in the words they would use, when it happened in UTC, who was affected, what you have done and will do with dates, and how to reach a person. It carries an apology, an explanation, or both, in an order your team chooses (Part 3). It does not carry internal names, other customers or a blow-by-blow timeline.

Real world

The bigger the hit, the more they want to read. Google's Customer Reliability Engineering team argues that sharing postmortems with customers can be good for both sides, and that the more of a customer's error budget an incident consumed, the more detail that customer wants. A short note may be right for a blip; an incident that ate a month's budget earns a full shared postmortem. (Hilton and Stockman, "Fearless shared postmortems", Google Cloud blog, 2017, read at source.)

Part 2 · covers what to say and what to redact

Redact people and systems, keep the why

5 min live

Redaction is not a vague "make it external". It is a line-by-line pass with three marks: keep, reword, redact. People and internal names come out. The reasoning, including the parts that are uncomfortable, stays close to how the team wrote it, because that is the part the customer is reading for.

LiveWhat comes out, what stays3 min▶

Google's CRE team gives a short list. Redact the names of people and use the role instead ("a network engineer"). Redact internal system names. Redact other customers' details, calling them Customer A and Customer B if they must appear. On graphs, keep the time axis and drop the absolute quantities, or switch to percentages.

Then the part teams get wrong: keep the "why did it happen" and "where we got lucky" sections close to as-is. Sanding the reasoning down until it says nothing is the fastest way to lose the customer's trust. For background, write the least detail that still explains the incident. On PM-11 that means the customer learns a price file arrived in the wrong unit and nothing checked the prices; they do not learn the validator's name or the runbook number.

Self-studyLegal and comms review · who signs, in what order3 min▶

A constructed sign-off order, shown so you can adapt it; this is a process sketch, not legal advice, and your own legal team sets the real rules:

  1. The postmortem owner drafts the public note from the reviewed team version, with the marked-up copy beside it.
  2. The incident lead checks every fact against the team version: no number, time or action may appear that the team version does not support.
  3. The comms or support lead reads it as a customer would: plain words, no internal names, a working contact.
  4. Legal reviews last among the editors, because their changes are the ones most likely to move the apology and the explanation.
  5. One named executive approves publication, and support gets the final text before customers do.

Write the order down in your docs standard (Leader session 1), so the second public note does not rediscover it under pressure.

Real world

Graphs keep their clock, lose their scale. The CRE post's advice on charts is a neat example of the general rule: the customer needs to see when things went wrong and how far, so the time axis stays, while the absolute quantities, which reveal internal scale, are dropped or turned into percentages. (Hilton and Stockman, "Fearless shared postmortems", Google Cloud blog, 2017, read at source.)

Part 3 · covers apology versus explanation, and timing

Sorry first, or why first?

4 min live

Two decisions shape every customer note: what goes first, and when it goes out. The second has guidance you can follow. The first does not: experienced people disagree, and the honest move is to show your team both positions and choose on purpose.

LiveThe apology argument, both camps2 min▶

Apologise first, and early. Some practitioners advise an explicit apology in the first lines, before any explanation: the customer was hurt, and an explanation that arrives before the apology reads as an excuse.

Explain first. Others put what happened and what you are doing about it first, with the apology after: customers want to know whether it will happen again, and an apology with no remediation behind it reads as empty.

And a third pull. Legal review often pulls toward saying less of both. The CRE post on shared postmortems does not discuss apologies at all. Vendor and comms blogs disagree with each other (reported). No measured study settles it. Your team's job is to choose an order on purpose, write the reason in one sentence, and keep it until a reader tells you otherwise.

LiveWhen each document can go out2 min▶

CRE separates two documents. An incident report can be written as soon as the author is confident in the cause. A shared postmortem waits for the internal one, so the public version is cut from something the team has already argued over. With automation and practice, CRE puts a shareable version at about 10% extra work plus review.

PagerDuty builds the external message into the postmortem meeting itself: after the owner walks through causes, timeline and actions, the customer liaison summarises customer impact and the external message is reviewed and approved in the room. Leader session 2 designed that meeting; this is the agenda item it hands to you.

Self-studyWhat the timing trade-off costs2 min▶

Going early buys trust and costs accuracy: an incident report written before the internal review may state a cause the review later revises. Going late buys accuracy and costs trust: customers fill a silence with their own story. A common pattern is both: a short dated incident report once the cause is clear, saying a fuller write-up will follow, then the shared postmortem after review. Security incidents carry external clocks of their own; see Cyber Security, the first hour ↗.

Real world

The external message has a seat at the review. PagerDuty's postmortem guide puts the customer liaison on the meeting agenda: they summarise the customer impact, and the external message is reviewed and approved before people leave. The note is not written after the meeting by whoever has time; it is a decision the meeting makes. (PagerDuty, Postmortem guide (Apache 2.0), docs.pagerduty.com, read at source 2026-11-11.)

Step 4 · Write Pellmoor's public note. Fill the template. Keep the s4 figures only if your note chooses to, and label them constructed in class. Put the apology sentence and the explanation sentence in the order your team chose in Part 3.

★ Step 4 · public note (constructed Pellmoor example)# Wrong prices in the Pellmoor app, 28 October 2026 What happened: ________________________________________ (in the customer's words, no internal names) When: from 09:40 UTC to 12:40 UTC on 28 October 2026. Who was affected: ______ (optional, constructed: 1,140 products; 2,310 orders placed; 412 orders refunded) [Sentence A, apology]: ________________________________________ [Sentence B, explanation]: ____________________________________ (Your order: A then B, or B then A. Reason: __________________ ) What we have done: prices restored at 12:40 UTC; affected orders refunded the difference. What we will do: by 18 November 2026 ____________ ; by 25 November 2026 ____________ . Questions: ____________ (a contact a customer can actually reach)

Step 5 · Paste your note into the linter and read it critically. Replace the text in the widget with your public note. Note which rules fire and whether they should. The owner rule will ask for an "Owner:" line a customer note should not carry. If your note has Impact or Actions headings, dated promises with no named owner read as wishes. If it has no Impact, Timeline or Actions heading at all, the linter treats it as a runbook. None of that makes the note wrong. A public note is judged by its readers, not by the linter.

★ Step 5 · what the linter said, and what you thinkRules that fired: ______________________________________ Fired and should be fixed: ____________________________ Fired but does not fit a customer note (and why): ______ Read as: postmortem / runbook (circle one) Who will read it before it ships: support lead, ____ , ____

Keep the marked-up copy. File the s4 marking beside the public note. When legal or comms asks why a line was cut or kept, the answer is already written, and the next incident's note starts from your marks instead of a blank page.
